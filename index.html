Imaging optics, from first principles to a lens you can actually buy

Optics is the part of a vision system people assume comes free with the camera. It doesn't. The same 5 MP sensor will read a date code reliably or fail intermittently depending entirely on the glass in front of it — and the two lenses will look identical on their datasheets.

This guide walks the whole chain: field of view, working distance, sensor size, resolution, contrast, MTF, depth of field, distortion, and perspective. Every idea has a diagram and a worked number.

Source material: “Imaging Optics Fundamentals” — Gregory Hollows, Director of Machine Vision Solutions, Edmund Optics.

01

The five parameters every imaging system has

Before touching a catalogue, five numbers have to exist. Four are things you decide from the application. The fifth — magnification — is what falls out of the other four, and it is not a fundamental parameter, even though vendors quote it like one.

ParameterDefinitionWho decides it
Field of view (FOV)The viewable area of the object — the part that fills the sensorThe application
Working distance (WD)Front of the lens to the object at best focus. Not total track.The machine / environment
ResolutionMinimum feature size you must resolve on the objectThe inspection spec
Depth of field (DOF)Object depth (or movement) that stays in acceptable focusPart handling tolerance

PMAGSensor size ÷ FOVDerived. Not fundamental.

The trade-off that runs through everything

The same lever that buys you resolution takes away depth of field. Opening the aperture for resolution costs DOF; closing it for DOF eventually costs resolution to diffraction. A security camera wants huge DOF and tolerates soft detail. A confocal microscope wants almost no DOF on purpose. Decide which end of that trade you live on before you shop.

02

Getting field of view from a lens datasheet

Roughly 70–80% of machine vision lenses are specified by focal length plus an angular field of view. That angle is quoted at infinity, measured from the principal plane inside the lens — not from the front housing you can actually put a ruler on. So the geometry below gets you close, never exact.

tan( 0.5 × angular FOV ) = ( 0.5 × FOV ) / WD
→ solve for FOV if you know the lens, or for the angle you need if you know the FOV

Working distance is a real design constraint

It is easy to treat WD as flexible. Often it isn't:

  • Lighting has to fit between the lens and the part — some geometries are large and want the light physically in that space.
  • Hot, caustic, or sealed environments force a long standoff, sometimes through a chamber window.
  • The opposite failure is more common: a tiny camera crammed close to a large part. Short WD plus wide FOV is optically hard, expensive, and hurts both lighting and resolution.

Rule of thumb

Aim to sit roughly 2–5× the object's size away from it. That leaves a lens designer room to hold real performance. It's a starting point, not a law.

Working distance also changes what your lighting does

A real case from the course: printing on the top of a can, inspected with a 12 mm lens up close. The lighting entered the lens at an angle that produced dark bands across the frame, so good prints were being rejected alongside bad ones. Nothing was wrong with the camera, the light, or the algorithm.

Swapping to a 75 mm lens at a longer working distance — same camera, same light, same family of lens, similar price — changed the ray angles entering the system and flattened the illumination profile. The problem disappeared, and the longer focal length also picked up scratches and watermarks the short lens could never resolve at that FOV.

03

Sensor “sizes” are a lie you have to memorise

A ½-inch sensor is not half an inch. Not the width, not the height, not the diagonal. The names are inherited from the outer diameter of the vidicon tubes these sensors replaced, kept so that old lenses would give roughly the old field of view. They persist because changing them would break every catalogue in the industry.

Image circle: the spec that bites you on a camera upgrade

A lens projects a circle of light. The sensor is a rectangle cut out of it. If the circle covers the rectangle, you're fine. Then the camera vendor drops in a physically larger sensor with the same pixel count — and your corners go dark. That's vignetting, and on a datasheet it appears as “maximum sensor coverage”.

Bigger pixels are not automatically worse

For the same pixel count, a larger sensor has larger pixels, which collect more light and give better signal-to-noise — usually better image quality overall. But it needs a lens that covers a much larger image circle, and that costs. Meanwhile shrinking pixels runs into the diffraction limit, below which the optics simply cannot deliver a smaller spot. Both ends have a wall.

04

Primary magnification is just a ratio

For fixed-magnification optics — telecentric lenses, microscope objectives — you skip the angular geometry entirely. PMAG is the ratio between the sensor and the field of view, and it maps your pixels onto the object.

FOV = Sensor size / PMAG
example — ½" sensor (6.4 mm horizontal) with a 0.5× lens
FOV = 6.4 / 0.5 = 12.8 mm
05

Resolution, line pairs, and the megapixel trap

Two dark squares sitting on adjacent pixels image as one wide rectangle. To call them two things, you need white space between them — and that space costs a pixel. So the unit of resolution is not the pixel, it's the line pair: one dark plus one light, two pixels minimum.

The calculation chain, end to end

frequency (lp/mm) = 1 / spacing (mm)
line pair = 2 × pixel
camera resolution (µm) = 2 × pixel size (µm)

object space resolution (µm) = image space resolution (µm) / PMAG
object space resolution (lp/mm) = PMAG × image space resolution (lp/mm)
FOV = sensor size / PMAG

This is where the maths stops

Everything above is the theoretical limit — what the sensor allows before any glass is involved. Every component you add takes something away. You cannot keep dividing: “I need 1 µm, so I'll buy a 10× lens on a system that resolves 10 µm” runs straight into physics. The rest of this guide is about how much of the theoretical number you actually get to keep.

06

Contrast: resolution's inseparable other half

Resolving a feature means the dark part reads dark and the light part reads light. How well they separate is contrast, and it is measured off the grayscale values in the image:

% Contrast = ( Imax − Imin ) / ( Imax + Imin )
DetectorContrast needed to call it “resolved”
CCD / CMOS imaging system10–20%
Human eye1–2%

That gap explains a conversation every vision engineer has had: “I can see the defect, why can't the camera?” Your eye works at a tenth of the contrast a camera needs, and your brain fills in repeating patterns it half-sees. If you have to tilt the part and move the light around to see a flaw, a camera will almost certainly fail on it.

Never judge contrast by eye on a monitor

Use software to measure grayscale values. Your visual system is an extremely good, extremely biased image processor — it will happily report detail that isn't in the data. Presentation software makes this worse: PowerPoint smooths and sharpens images on display, so what you see in a review deck is not what the algorithm receives.

The blue bunny suit

A pill inspection line separated red from green capsules on about 20 grayscale levels — workable, and it ran fine every day. Every time the boss walked the line, it started failing.

He wore a white coat; everyone else wore blue cleanroom suits. The reflection off his coat shifted the illumination just enough to push the threshold off. The fix wasn't a better algorithm or an enclosure — it was a $30 colour filter on a monochrome camera, which pushed the separation between the two capsule colours from ~20 levels to over 150. Contrast bought robustness that software could not.

07

MTF — the only spec that tells the truth

Because resolution only means something at a stated contrast, the useful specification is a curve, not a number. Modulation Transfer Function plots contrast against spatial frequency: how much of the object's contrast survives, at every level of detail.

Everything in the chain has an MTF

The lens, the sensor, the cables, the capture board, the monitor, your eye. A rough system estimate is the weakest link. The accurate answer is the product of all the individual MTFs — which is why a good camera plus a mediocre lens gives you a mediocre system, and multiplying two 60% components at your frequency of interest leaves 36%.

What real lenses look like across the field

Three lenses tested identically — 16 mm focal length, 1.5″ FOV, f/1.4, 0.157 PMAG, same target at 4 lp/mm object space, same lighting. Datasheets essentially indistinguishable.

CentreBottom middleCornerReading
Lens 159%56%62%Flat and strong everywhere. Trustworthy across the whole frame.
Lens 247%42%37%Steady roll-off to the corners. Predictable, but weaker.
Lens 352%22%36%Collapses mid-field, then recovers. Deceptive — a centre-only test passes it.

Lens 3's odd shape is a design choice, not a defect: selective vignetting. Stray rays that would blur the corners get clipped by an internal aperture, buying corner contrast at the cost of illumination and mid-field performance. It's a legitimate trade, and it will quietly ruin you if your feature happens to sit where the dip is.

The cost of getting this wrong

In the course's example, the price difference between the good lens and the poor one was a few hundred dollars on a roughly $15,000 system. Test at centre, mid-field and corner before you commit — and ask suppliers how the lens performs at your frequency, at your working distance, not just nominally.

What else moves the MTF

  • Wavelength. A lens corrected for white light performs differently under 660 nm red, and vice versa. Chromatic aberration comes in two flavours: axial (colours focus at different depths) and lateral (colours land at different heights off-axis).
  • Working distance. Push a lens far outside its design conjugate and the curve falls apart — a 6″ design run at 14″ can lose most of its contrast.
  • F/#. See the next section.
  • Aberrations and manufacturing tolerance. Two units of the same part number can differ if tolerance control is loose. Nominal specs don't capture this.
08

F/#: the one knob, and what it costs

The aperture is where you trade light, resolution and depth of field against each other. Closing it down blocks the badly-behaved rays that were blurring the image — so contrast improves — and it narrows the ray cone, which deepens focus. Both are good. Then diffraction takes over and everything gets worse.

Infinite conjugate F/# = focal length / diameter
Image-space close conjugate F/# = image distance / diameter
Object-space close conjugate F/# = object distance / diameter

smallest achievable spot = 1.22 × λ × F/#
at 520 nm: f/4 → 2.5 µm · f/8 → 5.1 µm · f/16 → 10.2 µm

lens diameter ∝ working distance / F/#

Points that catch people out

  • Two lenses with the same F/# share the same diffraction limit — and can still perform completely differently. F/# bounds the best case, it doesn't describe the lens.
  • Most lenses do not operate at their diffraction limit. Aberrations and tolerances get there first. Such a lens can be improved by stopping down, right up to the point it becomes diffraction limited.
  • DOF calculators usually assume diffraction-limited behaviour. If your lens isn't, the number is optimistic. Prefer testing with a 45° target at your actual resolution and contrast.
  • Changing F/# also changes relative illumination across the field, so your thresholds move.
  • Never state a DOF requirement alone. “Good depth of field” is not a spec. “±3 mm while holding 15 lp/mm at 20% contrast” is.
09

Distortion: misplaced, not lost

Distortion is a geometric aberration. A point lands somewhere other than where the ideal lens would put it — but no information is destroyed. That makes it the friendliest defect in this guide: if you know the mapping, software can put every point back.

% Distortion = ( AD − PD ) / PD × 100
AD = actual distance from field centre · PD = predicted distance with a perfect lens
  • Quoted distortion is usually the value at the extreme edge of the field. Since it varies with position, a plot of % distortion vs. distance from centre is far more useful than one number.
  • It's harder to correct in short focal length (wide angle) lenses — a 4.3 mm video lens can run past −20%.
  • Two lenses can quote 1% and 3% and the 1% one can look visibly more bent, because “distortion” alone doesn't say monotonic, symmetric, or measured how.
  • Ask about consistency across manufacturing. A calibration built on one unit has to survive the next unit.
10

Perspective error, and when to buy telecentric

Perspective error — parallax — is a change in magnification with a change in working distance. It's how your eyes judge depth: near things look bigger. For measurement, it's poison. A part sitting 1 mm higher on the conveyor measures larger, and no amount of calibration fixes it because the error depends on where the part happens to be.

The bonus nobody expects

Because magnification doesn't change with distance, a defocused telecentric image blurs symmetrically. The edge softens but the centroid stays put — so centre-position and hole-location measurements stay accurate even out of focus. A conventional lens blurs asymmetrically and drags the apparent centre with it.

The limitations, which are real

  • The field of view can never exceed the diameter of the front lens element. Inspecting a 100 mm part means a lens with a front element over 100 mm across — large, heavy, expensive.
  • Telecentricity is only specified over a limited range of working distances. Outside it, you have an ordinary lens.
  • Magnification is fixed, so a different FOV means a different lens, not a different mounting height.

Decision rule

If you are measuring dimensions on parts that have thickness, sit at varying heights, or move on a conveyor, budget for telecentric. If you are reading codes, checking presence, or finding defects, a good conventional lens is almost always the better spend.

The closing advice from the source

Optics processes images at the speed of light and costs a fraction of the software effort you'd spend compensating for it. Give it the time it deserves, specify a system rather than a parts list, and expect your optical supplier to know a great deal more than lens design.
